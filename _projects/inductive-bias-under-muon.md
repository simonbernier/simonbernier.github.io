> ***Status: in progress.***

One of my favorite days in the Iliad Intensive was the one on training dynamics, a field interested in studying how neural networks learn during training. **how does this relate to AI alignment**? Many of our hopes for alignment rest on the hope that LLMs have inductive biases and that somehow, alignment (whatever it means) could be a generally good thing to learn for a model to generalize out of distribution. Emergent misalignment seems to point in the direction that broadly misaligned behavior is easy to learn for models. 

Early work by Saxe ([Saxe et. al.]()) showed that deep linear networks (DLNs) initialized in the rich regime and trained with gradient descent GD learned a teacher matrix's singular values $s_j$ incrementally, on a typical time scale $\tau_j \sim 1/s_j$. We call these dynamics the saddle-to-saddle regime since the cricital points of a DLN's loss landscape are shown to be saddles except for the global minimum. This was explained by DLNs reaching a saddle point and silently aligning their subspace with the teacher matrix subspace incrementally, choosing the directions that most decrease the loss before modifying model weights in the direction of lower loss. This result was interpreted as a capacity for inductive bias, a form of Occam's razor, where simpler features are learned before more complex ones, which could explain how models generalize to never before seen data. Of course, gradient descent is not the only optimizer out there.

Modern open weight LLMs such as DeepSeek, Kimi and GLM train parts of their models with a new optimizer called Muon ([Muon,xxx](),[overview,xxx]()), which is a version of spectral GD. Muon does a gradient step to update model weights by first doing a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the gradient with the Newton-Schultz algorithm and setting all singular values to one. In a DLN, the training dynamics are solved exactly and Muon has the particularity of teaching a teacher matrix's singular values at the same rate, irrespective of the size of singular values. This causes the model to learn small singular values because fewer optimizer steps are necessary to reach them, while large singular values are learned late. This in fact completely reverses the inductive bias of GD and the saddle-to-saddle dynamics are lost. In fact, DLNs trained with Muon bypass saddle points entirely and follow a "shorter" trajectory directly to the global minimum, which explains why they train faster. To add a layer of complexity, biases cannot be updated with Muon because they are typically vectors for which an SVD does not exist. Those weights are instead trained with AdamW, an adaptive optimizer with momentum, which has its own training dynamics. On top of this, most optimizers use weight decay and learning rate scheduling, adding another layer of complexity.

Singular learning theory (SLT) relates the training dynamics of a Bayesian learner to that of a neural network trained with GD, named the Bayesian Antecedant Hypothesis. Early work showed that a Toy Model of Superposition (TMS) ([Anthropic,2022]()) showed phase transitions with saddle-to-saddle like dynamics and that decreases in loss were correlated in increases in the local learning coefficient (LLC). This is significant because a model learning in the rich regime has an inductive bias that lets it generalize well. In SLT, a loss landscape that is highly singular has high complexity and the singularity of the landscape is translated into a global parameter called the learning coefficient that is larger with more complexity. Therefore, it is encouraging to see the non-Bayesian equivalent (the LLC) showing increases while loss decreases. As the model learns more and more complex features, the landscape becomes more singular, more complex, which results in an increase of the LLC.

My project investigated if that picture persisted when training a model with Muon (and biases with GD/AdamW). Early results indicate that the Muon optimizer preserves the inductive bias of the data and mirrors the GD training methods, except that it trains faster. Therefore, certain phase transitions happen very fast and are difficult to resolve. The counterfactual is running the same experiment while removing the inductive bias in the TMS. There, the phase transitions vanish as Muon learns features at the same rate. **what happens with GD?**
